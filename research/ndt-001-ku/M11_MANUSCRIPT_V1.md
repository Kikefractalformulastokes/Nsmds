# Evidence-First Assurance of Ultrasonic Guided-Wave Damage Localization: Reproducibility, Blind Re-evaluation, Tail Risk, and Claim Control

**Affiliation:** CoreSyn — Independent Evidence & Model Assurance Lab

## Abstract
Data-driven non-destructive testing (NDT) can report strong average localization accuracy while concealing instability, distribution-shift sensitivity, and rare but consequential errors. We present an evidence-first assurance study using a public ultrasonic guided-wave dataset for delamination localization in a cross-ply composite beam. The analysis freezes source files by cryptographic digest, establishes a nearest-neighbour reference, evaluates generic continuous localization without post-hoc test tuning, performs paired uncertainty analysis and ablation, and then re-evaluates the frozen challenger on a deterministic internal blind subset. On the original noise-free alternating-position holdout (n=40), nearest-neighbour localization produced 15.000 mm mean absolute error (MAE), whereas inverse-square two-neighbour continuous interpolation produced 8.668 mm MAE, an observed 42.21% reduction. However, the paired bootstrap 95% interval for mean improvement was [-3.351, 13.978] mm and the challenger exhibited a 108.315 mm maximum error. Under a corrected deterministic internal blind re-split (n=16), the same generic challenger achieved 14.403 mm MAE versus 15.000 mm for the reference, only a 3.98% observed reduction, while retaining a 102.083 mm maximum error. Under the studied mixed-noise OOD condition, the generic continuous method also degraded relative to the nearest-neighbour reference. Ablation showed that the observed holdout improvement is attributable to generic continuous interpolation rather than to any canonical NS-MDS mechanism. These results illustrate why average benchmark improvement alone is insufficient evidence for operational NDT claims. We propose a reproducible assurance chain combining provenance, frozen evaluation, paired uncertainty, tail-risk reporting, ablation, blind re-evaluation, OOD stress testing, and explicit claim gates.

**Keywords:** non-destructive testing; ultrasonic guided waves; structural health monitoring; damage localization; model assurance; out-of-distribution; reproducibility; uncertainty; composite structures

## 1. Introduction
Ultrasonic guided waves are attractive for structural health monitoring because they can interrogate comparatively large structural regions from sparse sensing. Increasingly, data-driven localization methods are evaluated on simulated or experimental waveform datasets. Yet a low aggregate error on a benchmark does not by itself establish robustness, transferability, causal attribution, or fitness for operational inspection.

This distinction matters in safety-relevant NDT. A model may improve mean error while creating a small number of large localization failures. A result may also depend on a particular split, preprocessing choice, interpolation rule, or distribution regime. If these factors are not separated, an exploratory improvement can be overstated as evidence of a deployable system.

This work therefore asks a different question from conventional leaderboard evaluation: **what can the available evidence actually support?** We construct a staged assurance chain around a public ultrasonic guided-wave delamination dataset. The chain is intentionally designed to expose rather than hide uncertainty: source provenance is frozen; a simple reference is retained; candidate improvements are evaluated without test-label tuning; paired uncertainty and tail behavior are reported; ablation identifies the source of apparent lift; OOD behavior is inspected; and a deterministic internal blind re-split tests whether the headline effect persists under a changed evaluation partition.

The contributions are:
1. a reproducible, hash-pinned evaluation chain for a public guided-wave localization dataset;
2. demonstration that a large observed average improvement on one holdout can coexist with a confidence interval crossing zero and >100 mm tail errors;
3. a corrected internal blind re-evaluation showing substantial shrinkage of the apparent effect;
4. ablation evidence attributing the improvement to generic two-neighbour continuous interpolation, not to a proprietary or canonical NS-MDS mechanism; and
5. an explicit claim-control framework separating benchmark evidence from industrial, certification, or operational claims.

## 2. Data and provenance
We use the public dataset **Multiple ultrasonic-guided wave signals generated by wave propagation model: focused on delamination damage location variations in cross-ply composite beams**, released by Lu et al. through KU Leuven RDR (DOI: 10.48804/JAIG58). The dataset documentation describes transient finite-element guided-wave simulations for a 1 m × 3 mm × 2 mm cross-ply carbon-fibre composite beam with [0₂/90]s stacking and a 5 mm delamination. Excitation is a six-cycle Hanning-windowed 100 kHz sinusoid; the sensor is 0.2 m from excitation and the A0 mode is excited. Damage location is normalized from 0 to 1 over 30–70 cm in 5 mm increments.

The assurance run pins the source CSVs by SHA-256. The noise-free source used for the primary chain has SHA-256 `41a22d3e40a70938391e4b653e5a31765c2622237c1ff1a41e376eef15f0a8f0`; the averaged 10 dB ID source has SHA-256 `67c3be672c3212c6521f81b1865c30dd88769fae8c121ac66b86dd8ac8dbb71c`; and the mixed-noise OOD source has SHA-256 `cf67fc64f37aa040980ded6609a3516ed7d9020650a731b8b5092c8cec0591b9`.

Normalized damage position p is mapped to physical location as

**x_mm = 300 + 400 p.**

This mapping is used consistently in the corrected chain, including the blind re-evaluation.

## 3. Methods
### 3.1 Frozen reference evaluation
For the primary noise-free evaluation, alternating damage positions are assigned to training and holdout sets. The reference predictor is nearest-neighbour localization in raw waveform space. This intentionally simple baseline provides a transparent anchor and avoids conflating model complexity with assurance quality.

### 3.2 Generic continuous localization challenger
The principal challenger selects the two nearest training waveforms and estimates continuous location using inverse-square distance weighting. It is referred to as `knn2_inv2` / `knn2_cont` in the evidence chain. It is a **generic interpolation baseline** and is not represented as canonical NS-MDS.

### 3.3 Distribution-shift stress tests
The frozen methods are also evaluated on the supplied averaged 10 dB ID data and mixed-noise OOD data. No OOD test labels are used to tune the principal challenger. Exploratory adaptive routing experiments are retained as diagnostic evidence but are not promoted to validated operational rules.

### 3.4 Paired statistical analysis
For the primary n=40 holdout, per-sample absolute errors from the nearest-neighbour reference and frozen continuous challenger are paired. We compute the observed mean difference and a 20,000-replicate paired bootstrap interval using seed 20260827. We additionally report counts of better/worse cases and tail-error thresholds.

### 3.5 Ablation and attribution
Ablation compares nearest neighbour, uniform two-neighbour interpolation, inverse-distance two-neighbour interpolation, inverse-square two-neighbour interpolation, and a three-neighbour inverse-square variant. This gate is diagnostic: its purpose is to determine which generic component creates the observed improvement and which variants increase failure risk.

### 3.6 Corrected internal blind re-evaluation
A deterministic 20% subset of the 81 noise-free locations is held out using seed 20260827 before prediction (n=16). The remaining 65 samples form the training set. The exact public CSV schema and physical mapping `300 + 400p` are used. The frozen nearest-neighbour and inverse-square two-neighbour methods are then compared without tuning on blind labels.

This is an **internal blind re-split**, not independent external validation, not an aircraft test, and not third-party validation.

## 4. Results
### 4.1 Primary holdout
On the n=40 noise-free alternating-position holdout, the nearest-neighbour reference achieved 15.000 mm MAE. The generic inverse-square two-neighbour continuous method achieved 8.668 mm MAE, corresponding to an observed 6.332 mm mean improvement and 42.21% relative MAE reduction. The challenger was better on 36/40 samples and worse on 4/40.

The distribution of errors changes the interpretation. Challenger median error was 0.247 mm and P90 was 4.608 mm, but maximum error reached 108.315 mm. Three errors exceeded 30 mm. Thus the method combined excellent performance on most cases with a small set of large failures.

### 4.2 Paired uncertainty
The 20,000-replicate paired bootstrap interval for mean improvement (reference absolute error minus challenger absolute error) was **[-3.351, 13.978] mm**. The bootstrap probability of improvement greater than zero was 0.923. Because the 95% interval crosses zero, the experiment does not support a conventional 95%-confidence superiority claim despite the large observed MAE reduction.

### 4.3 Ablation
Ablation produced the following primary-holdout MAEs: nearest neighbour 15.000 mm; uniform two-neighbour interpolation 10.000 mm; inverse-distance two-neighbour interpolation 9.287 mm; inverse-square two-neighbour interpolation 8.668 mm; and inverse-square three-neighbour interpolation 43.904 mm. The three-neighbour variant generated 22 errors above 30 mm.

The pattern indicates that the apparent lift is explained by the generic two-neighbour continuous interpolation family. It does **not** establish causal attribution to canonical NS-MDS.

### 4.4 ID and OOD behavior
On the averaged 10 dB ID dataset, nearest-neighbour MAE was 7.407 mm and the two-neighbour continuous method achieved 4.637 mm. On the mixed-noise OOD dataset, however, nearest-neighbour MAE was 6.667 mm whereas the generic two-neighbour continuous method degraded to 19.575 mm, with a maximum error of 103.540 mm. A training-only adaptive routing experiment reduced this OOD MAE to 16.226 mm but remained worse than the simple nearest-neighbour reference. These stress tests show that improvement under the noise-free/ID regimes did not translate into robust OOD superiority.

### 4.5 Corrected internal blind re-evaluation
The corrected deterministic blind subset contained 16 of 81 noise-free samples. The nearest-neighbour reference again produced 15.000 mm MAE. The frozen challenger produced **14.403 mm MAE**, an observed **3.98%** relative reduction. It was better on 12/16 cases and worse on 4/16. Median challenger error was 0.406 mm, but P90 was 49.383 mm and maximum error was 102.083 mm.

The direction of the mean effect persisted, but its magnitude shrank sharply from 42.21% on the original holdout to 3.98% on the internal blind re-split. Tail risk persisted. This result is central to the assurance interpretation: a compelling headline metric was not stable in magnitude under a changed evaluation partition.

## 5. Discussion
The principal finding is not that one localization algorithm is universally better than another. Rather, the evidence chain demonstrates how an apparently strong benchmark result changes when subjected to progressively stricter assurance gates.

First, aggregate MAE alone was incomplete. The two-neighbour challenger reduced MAE strongly on the original holdout because most predictions were extremely accurate, yet it produced rare errors above 100 mm. In inspection contexts, these tail events may be more consequential than modest changes in average error.

Second, statistical uncertainty moderated the headline result. The observed 42.21% MAE reduction was not accompanied by a paired 95% interval wholly above zero. Reporting only the point estimate would therefore overstate the evidence.

Third, ablation prevented attribution inflation. The observed improvement survived across several generic two-neighbour interpolation variants and failed dramatically when extended to three neighbours. The appropriate conclusion is that a simple interpolation mechanism explains the effect; the study provides no basis for attributing the improvement to canonical NS-MDS.

Fourth, the corrected internal blind re-split exposed split sensitivity. Although the challenger remained slightly better in mean MAE, the effect contracted to 3.98% and large errors remained. This is exactly the type of information that should be known before a model is presented as robust.

Finally, the OOD results demonstrate that in-distribution improvement does not imply resilience to changed noise regimes. The simple reference outperformed the continuous challenger on the studied OOD set. A production assurance program should therefore define explicit operating envelopes, abstention or fallback policies, independent datasets, and failure-oriented acceptance criteria before deployment.

## 6. Claim-control framework
Based on this evidence, the following statements are supportable:
- the public benchmark and source provenance were independently reproduced in a pinned computational chain;
- generic two-neighbour continuous interpolation reduced observed MAE on the original noise-free holdout;
- the magnitude of that improvement was not stable under the corrected internal blind re-split;
- the challenger exhibited material tail errors and degraded under the studied OOD noise condition;
- ablation attributes the observed lift to generic interpolation rather than canonical NS-MDS.

The following statements are **not** supported by this study:
- that NS-MDS caused the reported localization improvement;
- that the challenger is superior at conventional 95% confidence on the primary holdout;
- that the method has been independently or externally validated;
- that it has demonstrated performance on operational aircraft inspections;
- that it is certified, regulator-approved, airline-approved, or ready for safety-critical deployment.

## 7. Limitations
The dataset is generated from finite-element simulations rather than a new independent experimental campaign. The primary holdout and corrected blind re-split draw from the same underlying public noise-free dataset, so the blind gate tests split sensitivity rather than external generalization. Sample sizes are modest, especially for the mixed-noise OOD set. The interpolation methods are deliberately simple and are not intended as state-of-the-art damage-localization architectures. The study does not evaluate defect detection probability, sizing accuracy, probability of detection curves, sensor placement uncertainty, environmental variability, manufacturing variability, inspection procedure effects, or human factors required for industrial qualification.

## 9. Conclusion
A strong average benchmark result is not equivalent to assured NDT performance. In this public guided-wave localization case, a generic continuous interpolation method reduced observed MAE from 15.000 to 8.668 mm on the original holdout, yet its paired 95% improvement interval crossed zero, errors above 100 mm occurred, OOD performance degraded, and the effect contracted to 3.98% under a corrected internal blind re-split. Ablation further showed that the apparent lift arose from generic interpolation rather than a proprietary mechanism.

The broader result is methodological: provenance, frozen baselines, paired uncertainty, tail-risk analysis, ablation, blind re-evaluation, OOD stress testing, and explicit claim gates materially change what can responsibly be concluded from an NDT benchmark. Evidence-first assurance should precede operational claims.
